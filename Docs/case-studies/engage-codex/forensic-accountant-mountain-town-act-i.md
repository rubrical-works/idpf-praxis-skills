# Case Study: Forensic accountant returns to a dying mountain town — Act I structural exploration

**Skill:** [engage-codex](../../../Skills/engage-codex/)
**Run date:** 2026-05-18
**Paths explored:** 3
**Format:** feature-screenplay
**Recommended path:** Path 3 (Kishōtenketsu scene outline)

---

## Framing preamble

This case study captures a real run of `engage-codex` against a structural problem that is familiar to anyone outlining a moral-grey literary thriller: **how do you stage the first act of a story whose central revelation is mid-act and whose antagonist is also the protagonist's only remaining family — without collapsing the tone into either procedural ("who did it?") or melodrama ("how could you?")**.

The question fits `engage-codex` because:

- It is **generative**, not analytical — the answer is a designed artefact (a beat sheet, a scene outline) that a screenwriter could critique and revise. There is no research conclusion to reach.
- It has **multiple genuinely-live structural paradigms** (three-act, Save-the-Cat, Kishōtenketsu) that produce structurally different artefacts for *this specific brief*. The brief's defining feature — the act-I-break is a *decision* rather than an event — stress-tests the assumptions baked into each paradigm.
- The recommended artefact needs a **structural critique** before adoption, because the dominant failure mode of an Act I that contains its own mid-act revelation is pacing collapse (everything interesting front-loaded) and unsupported tonal shift (literary thriller drifting into procedural).

**Web citations are N/A.** The skill is offline-only and generative; the artefact *is* the output, not a synthesis of cited sources. Reference works named in beats (*The Place Beyond the Pines*, *Ozark*) are tonal anchors supplied in the brief, not citations. This is explicit in the skill contract — narrative work is structurally creative and does not run a research layer.

**What to look for:**

- All three paths produce **full structured artefacts** with concrete beat/scene content — pure-prose paths fail the skill contract.
- Synthesis is a **comparison table**, not a merged outline. Different narrative paradigms are not commensurable along a single structural axis — there is no graft mechanism (in contrast to `engage-forge`).
- The **narrative critique pass is mandatory and runs on every path**, covering four categories (pacing valleys, unset-up payoffs, character-motivation gaps, unsupported tonal shifts). Each category is populated with concrete findings tied to beat/scene numbers OR explicit `*Clean.*`.
- The recommended path emerges from how well the paradigm handles a **mid-act revelation whose payload is moral rather than informational** — not from which paradigm is "best" in the abstract.

---

## Problem

> Outline the first act of a feature screenplay about a forensic accountant who is recalled to the small mountain town she grew up in to investigate why the local credit union's books reconcile perfectly while the town's small businesses are quietly going bankrupt — and who uncovers, partway through the act, that her estranged sister, who manages the credit union, has built an elaborate compassion-driven fraud structure to keep the dying town alive. Target format: feature screenplay (~110 pages, this is the first ~25 pages / Act I). Tone: literary thriller with moral-grey emphasis (think *The Place Beyond the Pines* meets *Ozark*, not *Knives Out*).

## Artefacts

### Path 1 — Three-act beat sheet

**Paradigm rationale.** Three-act structure forces explicit placement of the inciting incident, midpoint reversal, and act break — which directly stress-tests where the sister-reveal lands in this premise. The recommended placement of "mid-act-I revelation with the act break being Cara's *decision*" is structurally unusual and must survive the classical paradigm's expectations to be defensible.

Visual: fluorescents, beige folders, no view through windows. |

She calls her supervisor's voicemail and starts to leave a message, then hangs up. She goes to the credit union after hours. Maren is still there. They sit across the desk where Cara was working. Cara does not show her notes. Maren does not ask. They talk about their mother for the first time in five years. Neither names what they both know Cara has found. | Two-hander scene at the heart of the moral-grey premise. Neither sister moves. The structural tension is unresolved by design. |
| 10 | Act I break — the decision, withheld | 24–25 | Cara drives back to her motel at 3 a.m. She opens her laptop. The cursor blinks in the empty "Report Findings" field. She looks at it for a long time. Cut to black. **The decision is not shown.** | **Act I break.** The break is Cara's withheld decision — the audience does not see what she chooses. Act II will open on the consequence of the decision, not the decision itself. |

**Rationale.** The strongest move is placing the sister-reveal at beat 7 (page ~17, mid-act-I) rather than at the act break — this matches the brief's specification and makes the act break a *moral decision* rather than an informational discovery. The structure earns its third act by spending Act I on three nested escalations (procedural anomaly → structural pattern → personal weight) rather than one. **Most contestable assumption:** that the audience will accept an Act I break with no externally visible action — a withheld decision risks reading as anticlimax in a genre (thriller) that conventionally breaks on event. **Tradeoff:** classical three-act expects the act break to redirect the protagonist's external pursuit, but here Cara's external pursuit (the audit) is unchanged at the break — only her internal stance has moved. This buys moral-grey tonal coherence at the cost of structural visibility.

**What would change this artefact:**
- If the producer or studio target is a more conventional thriller (closer to *Knives Out* than *Ozark*), the Act I break must contain a visible external action (e.g., Cara sends the report, or she destroys her notes) and the withheld-decision move fails.
- If the screenplay is intended as the first hour of a limited series rather than a feature first act, beats 7 and 8 can each expand into their own episode and the revelation rhythm slows considerably.
- If the brief shifts to a single-POV first-person voice (Cara as voiceover narrator), beat 9 must give Cara internal monologue and the silent two-hander loses its weight.

### Path 2 — Save-the-Cat beat sheet

**Paradigm rationale.** Save-the-Cat's beat density across the first quarter of a feature (Opening Image, Theme Stated, Set-Up, Catalyst, Debate, Break into Two) is tested against the 25-page constraint and supplies an explicit Theme Stated beat where the moral-grey premise can be planted early. The risk is that Save-the-Cat's beat-naming carries genre conventions ("Catalyst", "Break into Two") that pull the tone toward conventional thriller and away from literary moral-grey.

| 2 | Theme Stated | Theme Stated | 3 | Cara's supervisor, handing her the Halewood file: *"You always say the books don't care who's reading them. Let's see if that holds when you know the people."* Cara doesn't answer. **Theme: the books tell the truth, but the truth is not the whole answer.** |

Maren hands Cara a key to the back office and a stack of ledgers — exactly what a cooperating institution would do. Cara sees Maren's wedding ring is gone (it wasn't gone at the funeral). | 6 | (continuing) Debate | Debate (closes) | 17–22 | Cara watches Maren slip an envelope to a contractor at dawn. She understands the motive. She drafts a report and does not send it. She calls her supervisor and does not leave a message. The Debate beat in Save-the-Cat asks *"should the hero do the thing?"*; here it asks *"should the hero name what she has already found?"*. |
| 7 | (continuing) Debate | Debate (climaxes) | 22–24 | Cara goes to the credit union after hours. The two-hander scene with Maren — neither names what Cara has found, both know. They speak instead about their mother. Neither cries. The scene ends with Cara leaving without her notes. |
| 8 | Break into Two | Break into Two | 24–25 | Cara drives to the motel. Opens her laptop. The cursor blinks in the empty report field. The screen goes dark. Cut to act II. **The "Break into Two" is the unresolved decision itself**, which is structurally unconventional for Save-the-Cat — the break usually shows the hero committing to an external course. |

**Rationale.** The strongest move is using the Theme Stated beat (beat 2) to plant the moral-grey thesis explicitly in the supervisor's dialogue — Save-the-Cat is unusual among paradigms in *requiring* an early theme statement, and that requirement is a gift for a moral-grey premise. **Most contestable assumption:** that the Debate beat can absorb the sister-reveal at its midpoint. Save-the-Cat's Debate is conventionally about the *protagonist's* deliberation, not about an informational revelation; stretching it to contain the reveal risks the beat reading as bloated. **Tradeoff:** the paradigm's beat names ("Catalyst", "Break into Two") carry genre expectations from the screenwriting tradition Save-the-Cat codified — sticking to the beat names may pull the tone toward conventional thriller against the literary-moral-grey target.

**What would change this artefact:**
- If the screenplay's target festival/distribution profile is more commercial (Sundance Midnight rather than Sundance NEXT), the beat names become an asset rather than a liability and Save-the-Cat is the right pick.
- If the brief weakens the "estranged sister" relationship to "former colleague" or "old friend", the Theme Stated beat has nothing to grip and Save-the-Cat's machinery turns generic.
- If the page-budget for Act I expands to 30 pages, the Debate beat can split cleanly into Debate-A (information) and Debate-B (decision), resolving the bloated-midpoint concern.

### Path 3 — Kishōtenketsu scene outline

**Paradigm rationale.** Kishōtenketsu's `ten` is a *reframing* twist — the development does not redirect the protagonist's external pursuit, it changes what the preceding setup *means*. This maps the brief's central move exactly: the sister-reveal does not change what Cara is investigating, it changes what the investigation *is about*. The paradigm is also tonally permissive of the literary register — Kishōtenketsu does not require external conflict at every beat, which suits the moral-grey premise.

**Rationale.** The strongest move is the alignment of `ten` with the brief's mid-act revelation — Kishōtenketsu is the only one of the three paradigms whose central structural beat *requires* reframing rather than redirection, which means the sister-reveal lands as the paradigm's load-bearing move rather than as a beat the paradigm has to absorb. The retrospective fold of scene 7's casserole moment onto scene 8's revelation is paradigmatically native, not a writerly trick. **Most contestable assumption:** that a feature-screenplay audience trained on three-act will read the `ten` as a turning point and not as a stall. Kishōtenketsu's structural rhythm is non-native to most mainstream Western feature audiences and risks reading as "Act I ended without anything happening" if the reframing is not staged with sufficient visual weight. **Tradeoff:** the paradigm's tonal permissiveness — `ketsu` does not require external resolution — is a perfect fit for the moral-grey premise but reduces the artefact's immediate readability for industry readers expecting Save-the-Cat beats on a coverage card.

**What would change this artefact:**
- If the project is being pitched to a buyer or development executive trained on Save-the-Cat coverage (most mainstream studio/network development), Path 3's structural illegibility on a coverage card becomes a packaging problem and Path 2's beat names become a sales asset.
- If the brief tightens the genre target toward "thriller" with less moral-grey latitude, the `ten`-as-reframing move loses force because the audience expectation shifts toward redirection (who did it / what now) and Kishōtenketsu's permissive `ketsu` reads as unresolved.
- If scene 7 (Maren's kitchen) is cut for runtime or pacing in revision, the retrospective fold at scene 8 fails — the entire `ten` payoff in this outline depends on scene 7's casserole exchange being on screen and unexplained when it occurs.

## Synthesis (comparison, not merger)

| Path | Paradigm | Artefact | Strengths | Weaknesses | Best-served story shape | Fit with stated tone (moral-grey literary thriller) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Three-act | Beat sheet (10 beats) | Maximum structural legibility; explicit setup → inciting incident → act break placement; mid-act revelation at beat 7 is defensible against the paradigm's expectations. | The act break is a withheld decision, which is structurally unusual for three-act and risks reading as anticlimax in industry coverage. | Mainstream thrillers and dramas where the act break is conventionally an external decision the audience watches. | **Solid.** Defends the literary register against three-act's redirection bias, but the bias remains a structural headwind. |
| 2 | Save-the-Cat | Beat sheet (8 beats) | Theme Stated beat is a gift for the moral-grey thesis; beat density is well-tested against ~25-page Act I; instantly legible on coverage. | The Debate beat absorbs both the reveal and the deliberation, risking bloat at pages 10–22 — a six-page Debate that contains the reveal at its midpoint is a structurally crowded beat. | 3 | Kishōtenketsu | Scene outline (12 scenes) | `ten` aligns natively with the mid-act reframing — the sister-reveal *is* the paradigm's load-bearing move, not an absorbed surprise; tonal permissiveness suits moral-grey; the retrospective fold of scene 7 onto scene 8 is paradigmatically native. | Structural illegibility on a Save-the-Cat-trained coverage card; the `ketsu` does not visibly resolve plot, which can read as "Act I ended without anything happening" to an audience trained on three-act. | Literary thrillers, character pieces, and moral-grey work where the structural unit is *what changes meaning* rather than *what changes pursuit*. | **Strongest tonal fit.** The paradigm and the brief are built for each other. |

**Recommended:** **Path 3 (Kishōtenketsu scene outline).** The brief's central structural problem is that the act-I revelation must reframe rather than redirect, and Kishōtenketsu is the only paradigm whose `ten` beat is built for exactly that move. The tonal permissiveness of `ketsu` (resolution without external closure) maps directly onto the moral-grey, *Place Beyond the Pines* register the brief specifies. Path 1 (three-act) is a defensible runner-up if industry-coverage legibility is a packaging requirement; Path 2 (Save-the-Cat) is the right pick only if the project is repositioned toward a more commercial thriller.

## Narrative critique pass

The critique pass walks every path's artefact against four categories — pacing valleys, unset-up payoffs, character-motivation gaps, unsupported tonal shifts — with findings tied to specific beat or scene numbers, or an explicit `*Clean.*` declaration.

### Path 1 — Three-act beat sheet

**Pacing valleys.**
- **Beat 3 (Setup of the world Cara left, pages 4–7).** Three pages of backstory establishment immediately after the inciting-incident setup risks a soft stretch before the first real plot motion at beat 4. The montage device is efficient but front-loads delivery of information the audience has not yet earned the need for. *Remediation: compress to two pages, or fold half the setup into a single scene at the credit union where Cara has to explain her absence to Maren on the page.*

**Unset-up payoffs.**
- **Beat 8 (Confirmation in the wild — Cara watches Maren and the contractor, pages 18–21).** Cara's *visceral rather than analytical* understanding of Maren's motive is asserted without on-page evidence that Cara has access to enough of the town's circumstances to interpret an envelope exchange this way. The audience needs at least one earlier moment of Cara seeing Maren behave in this register so that the dawn scene is recognition, not exposition. *Remediation: plant a small moment in beat 6 (Maren's dinner) where Cara sees Maren handle a similar transaction with the casserole-bringing neighbour.*

**Character-motivation gaps.** *Clean.* Cara's competence-at-distance is established beat 1, her susceptibility to local-knowledge framing is established beat 2, and her refusal to confront Maren in beats 6 and 9 is consistent with her professional self-image as someone whose tool is *not* personal proximity.

**Unsupported tonal shifts.**
- **Beat 6 (Dinner at Maren's, pages 13–15).** The shift from procedural-tense (beat 5, back office) to intimate-domestic (beat 6, kitchen) lands inside a single page-turn without an interstitial breath. Three-act's beat-driven pacing accelerates the transition further. *Remediation: insert a brief exterior beat between 5 and 6 — Cara walking from the credit union to Maren's house at dusk — to let the tonal register reset before the kitchen scene.*

### Path 2 — Save-the-Cat beat sheet

**Pacing valleys.**
- **Beats 5–7 (Debate, pages 10–24).** The Debate beat carries the sister-reveal, the dawn confirmation, and the after-hours two-hander — twelve to fourteen pages of a single named beat. Save-the-Cat's beat density is designed for *one beat per slot*; here the Debate slot is doing the work of three beats and risks reading as a long middle stretch rather than as escalation. *Remediation: in revision, sub-label Debate-A (information), Debate-B (motive confirmation), Debate-C (confrontation), and stage explicit structural markers — a scene cut, a time jump, a location change — between them so the page-count weight reads as movement.*

**Unset-up payoffs.**
- **Beat 4 (Catalyst — Maren's missing wedding ring).** The detail of Maren's missing wedding ring is planted at the catalyst and is not retrieved anywhere in Act I. In Save-the-Cat's tightly-budgeted Act I, every catalyst-beat detail is structurally expected to pay off by the Break into Two. *Remediation: either pay off the ring (e.g., Cara mentions it in beat 7 and Maren refuses to discuss) or remove the detail from beat 4 and bank it for later acts.*

**Character-motivation gaps.**
- **Beat 2 (Theme Stated — supervisor's dialogue).** The supervisor's *"the books don't care who's reading them"* line as Theme Stated is *thematically* on target but is delivered by a character we never see again. ### Path 3 — Kishōtenketsu scene outline

**Pacing valleys.**
- **Scenes 4–6 (mountain road, Main Street walk, back office, pages 6–15).** Nine pages of `shō` (development) without an explicit interpersonal scene risks reading as travelogue if the visual atmosphere is not carrying weight. Kishōtenketsu's structural permissiveness here is also its risk — `shō` does not *require* escalation, so the burden falls entirely on the production design and Cara's interior life. *Remediation: ensure the hardware-store-owner cameo in scene 5 lands as a real beat (not a flyby) so that scenes 4 and 6 have an interpersonal anchor between them.*

**Unset-up payoffs.**
- **Scene 8 (the `ten` — pattern resolution, pages 17–19).** The retrospective fold of scene 7's casserole exchange onto scene 8's revelation depends on scene 7 being on screen and unexplained when it occurs. If a future revision compresses scene 7 (e.g., the neighbour's question is heard but Maren's reply is offscreen), the `ten` loses its native setup and reads as a discovery rather than a reframing. *Remediation: tag scene 7's casserole exchange as a load-bearing setup in the outline so it is not cut in revision; the scene's surface content (warmth, awkward dinner) is not its structural role.*

**Character-motivation gaps.**
- **Scene 2 (regulator's office — Cara accepts the file).** Cara's acceptance of the Halewood audit without explicit deliberation is structurally efficient but motivationally thin. **Unsupported tonal shifts.** *Clean.* Kishōtenketsu's tonal permissiveness is the paradigm's specific gift here — the shifts from sterile (scene 1) to bucolic (scene 4) to intimate (scene 7) to recognition (scene 8) are paradigmatically supported rather than fought.

## What would change this recommendation

1. **Industry-coverage packaging is the constraint.** If the project's path to production runs through development executives or readers whose coverage cards are Save-the-Cat-trained, Path 3's structural illegibility becomes a packaging problem. The recommended path would shift to Path 1 (three-act) for legibility on the coverage card, with the moral-grey weight defended in execution rather than in structure. Path 2 (Save-the-Cat) only becomes the recommendation if the project is also repositioned toward a more commercial thriller register.
2. **Genre target narrows to thriller (less moral-grey latitude).** If a revised brief tightens the tone toward conventional thriller — closer to *Knives Out* than *Ozark* — the `ten`-as-reframing move loses force because audience expectation shifts toward redirection (who did it / what now). Path 1 becomes the recommendation, and the act break must contain a visible external action (Cara sends the report, Cara destroys her notes) rather than the withheld decision currently staged at beat 10 / scene 12.
3. **Page budget for Act I changes.** If Act I expands to 30 pages, Path 2's bloated Debate beat resolves cleanly (Debate-A / Debate-B split), and Save-the-Cat's coverage legibility plus its Theme Stated beat make it competitive with Path 3. If Act I compresses to 20 pages, Path 3's nine-page `shō` stretch (scenes 4–6) is no longer affordable and Path 1's denser beat sheet becomes the only viable structure.
