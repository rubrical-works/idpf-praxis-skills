# CHORUS — Engineering-Budget Reallocation, Four-VP Mediation

## Framing

**Who would convene this:** A CEO at a ~1,500-person enterprise-software company facing a budget-cycle deadlock between four executive stakeholders, each with credible claims on the same engineering-budget pool. The CEO has signalled she wants a negotiated proposal rather than a tiebreaker request, which is exactly the structurally-distinct slot `engage-chorus` occupies: N-party steel-manning plus a named mediator's landscape — not a winner.

**Why the answer matters:** Real-world enterprise budget disputes routinely collapse into "loudest VP wins" or "split-the-difference" outcomes that nobody owns. The mediator-landscape framing forces every stakeholder's strongest reasonable form to be put on the table, identifies whether a ZOPA even exists, and produces a concession-sequencing plan that names what each move is intended to test about the deal space. Reading the case study, you should be able to predict where each stakeholder's reservation point bites, see which coalitions are mutual and which are asymmetric, and identify the unresolved conflicts that the rank 1 settlement papers over rather than retires.

**What to look for in the output:**
- **Steel-man quality** in each stakeholder brief — does the rationale actively reject the strawman, or just describe the strawman in nicer words?
- **The mediator landscape vs. the individual briefs** — the landscape is *not* a synthesis or a winner-picking. ZOPA, trade frontier, settlement zones, unresolved conflicts, concession sequencing, and coalition map are six structurally different views on the same room. Reading them together produces something none of the four briefs could produce alone.
- **Asymmetric coalitions** — the coalition map flags pairs where one brief signals alignment and the other doesn't reciprocate. These are the deal's fragility points.
- **The unresolved conflicts** — the landscape names five things rank 1 doesn't actually resolve. A CEO reading this can decide whether to ratify rank 1 with the conflicts hanging or push the room toward rank 2/3, which trade certainty for execution runway.

## Situation

> A ~1,500-person enterprise software company is in its FY26 budget cycle. Four stakeholders compete for the same engineering-budget pool with sharply different theses:
>
> - The **Platform Engineering VP** wants to fund a $4M/year service-mesh + observability stack migration (the company currently runs three competing in-house solutions and the operational toll is rising — incident MTTR trending the wrong way, senior platform attrition risk, product teams routing around the platform org).
> - The **Product Engineering VP** wants the same budget redirected to a feature-acceleration program to defend a flat-lining new-ACV growth rate. Sees feature delivery velocity as the lever she controls; sales and CS are citing a competitive feature gap as the proximate cause of stalled enterprise deals.
> - The **CFO** has mandated 15% YoY infrastructure cost reduction enterprise-wide. The mandate is not posturing — board pressure on operating margin is real, the company is in a post-IPO margin-improvement phase, and the CFO is accountable to investors for the cost trajectory.
> - The **new VP of Engineering** (in role ~60-90 days) wants to consolidate all platform-adjacent budgets under a single platform-of-platforms governance model in 2026. Sees the dispute itself as evidence of the governance gap and wants to use it as a forcing function for the consolidation.
>
> No party has positional authority over the others. The decision goes to the CEO, who has signalled she wants a negotiated proposal not a tiebreaker request.

## Stakeholders

### the-platform-vp

*Platform Engineering VP confronting an accelerating reliability-debt curve who sees consolidation onto a unified service-mesh and observability stack as the only durable way to stop the company taxing every product team with platform entropy.*

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| interests | • Halt the compounding operational toll of running three competing in-house solutions — on-call burnout, duplicated SRE headcount, incident MTTR trending the wrong way for several quarters<br>• Recover platform engineering credibility with product teams who currently route around the platform org because internal tooling is inconsistent and unreliable<br>• Establish a defensible architectural baseline before the company scales to the next revenue tier, where the cost of migration grows non-linearly with traffic and tenant count<br>• Retain senior platform talent who are visibly fatigued by maintaining bespoke forks and will leave (taking institutional knowledge) if the org signals it will not invest in foundational work<br>• Convert opaque, embedded platform costs (incident response, duplicated infra spend, slowed feature delivery) into a single legible line item that the CFO and CEO can actually reason about |
| statedPositions | Fund the full $4M/year service-mesh and observability stack migration as a multi-year platform investment, beginning this fiscal year, with platform engineering as the accountable owner. |
| batna | Without funding, the platform org continues triaging the three competing stacks in maintenance mode: incident volume keeps climbing, 2–3 senior platform engineers depart within 6–12 months, product teams quietly stand up their own fourth and fifth observability solutions to escape the pain, and the company arrives at the next scale tier with a worse architecture and higher unit economics — at which point a forced migration costs materially more than $4M and ships under duress after a customer-visible outage. |
| reservationPoint | A phased program with at least ~$2.4M/year of committed funding (roughly 60% of ask), an explicit multi-year roadmap with milestone-gated tranches, written executive endorsement of consolidation as the target architecture, and a hard commitment to sunset at least one of the three legacy stacks within 12 months. Anything below this is indistinguishable from being told 'no' while being asked to absorb the operational consequences anyway. |
| tradeableConcessions | • Phase the rollout over 24–30 months instead of 18, deferring roughly $1.2M of Y1 spend into Y2 in exchange for guaranteed multi-year commitment<br>• Accept measurable, externally auditable SLOs (MTTR reduction targets, on-call page volume, deploy frequency uplift) as gating criteria for releasing later funding tranches<br>• Open the new platform to product-org input on the migration sequence — let the product VP nominate which product surfaces migrate first to unblock their roadmap<br>• Absorb 1–2 headcount slots within the existing platform org rather than requesting net-new hiring, by reallocating engineers currently maintaining the deprecated stacks<br>• Commit to a public TCO scorecard the CFO co-owns, showing duplicated-infra savings and incident-cost reduction quarter over quarter, with a clawback clause if savings don't materialize<br>• Defer the premium observability tier (full distributed tracing across all services) to Year 2, shipping Year 1 with a leaner ingestion footprint to reduce vendor commit risk |
| coalitionSignals | • *the-new-vp-of-engineering* — A new engineering VP inherits whatever architectural debt exists today and will be measured on velocity and reliability outcomes; a consolidated platform is the single biggest lever for both, and gives them a credible 'first 180 days' narrative they can own jointly with platform<br>• *the-cfo* — The CFO's deeper interest is predictable unit economics and a defensible cost story to the board; converting three opaque, duplicated infra spends plus incident-driven overtime into one legible line item with measurable TCO trajectory is closer to the CFO's worldview than to a pure product-feature pitch, provided the ask is structured with gated tranches and a scorecard<br>• *the-product-vp* — Product velocity is currently throttled by platform inconsistency — every cross-cutting feature pays a tax in integration work; if platform offers the product VP migration-sequencing influence and an SLO that improves deploy frequency, the product org gains roadmap throughput it cannot buy any other way |

The temptation here is to caricature the platform VP as an empire-builder asking for a vanity rebuild — "three stacks work fine, just pick one for free" — and to frame the $4M as discretionary infrastructure indulgence competing with revenue work. The strongest reasonable form refuses that framing. The platform VP is not asking for a greenfield rewrite; they are pricing the already-accruing cost of three divergent stacks (duplicated headcount, rising incident toll, attrition risk, product velocity drag) and proposing to convert that hidden tax into a budgeted, milestone-gated program with measurable SLO outcomes and CFO-auditable TCO. That position is strongest when it (a) acknowledges the ask is large and offers genuine phasing, scope, and accountability concessions, (b) reframes the decision as "pay now in a controlled program or pay more later under duress," and (c) shows a clear path to coalition with both the incoming engineering VP (who needs this to succeed in role) and the CFO (whose real interest is predictable economics, not minimum spend). Steel-manning means treating the platform VP as a serious operator making a defensible capital-allocation argument, not as a cost center asking for a toy.

**Steel-man tier:** *strong*

### the-product-vp

*Revenue-accountable product engineering leader who reads flat new-ACV as an existential signal and views feature velocity as the only lever she controls on a quarterly horizon.*

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| interests | • Restore a credible new-ACV growth trajectory before the board's next planning cycle, because flat top-of-funnel growth at this scale invites valuation compression and forced cost actions that hurt everyone at the table<br>• Close a competitive feature-parity gap that sales and CS are citing as the proximate cause of stalled deals and elongated sales cycles<br>• Preserve product engineering's credibility as the team that can convert investment into shippable, demoable, sellable capability on a quarter-by-quarter cadence<br>• Reduce the cycle time from customer-validated problem to GA so that win/loss feedback can actually steer the roadmap inside a fiscal year<br>• Protect her team from a morale collapse driven by repeated reprioritization, attrition of senior ICs to competitors, and the perception that platform and finance interests outrank customer-facing delivery |
| statedPositions | Redirect the shared engineering budget pool to a feature-acceleration program — net new squads, design partner motion, and a 2-quarter delivery push targeting the top sales-blocking gaps — to defend new-ACV growth this fiscal year. |
| batna | If no deal is reached, she escalates to the CEO with a sales-and-CS-cosigned memo framing flat ACV as a revenue emergency, requests an emergency reallocation outside the negotiated pool, and in parallel quietly contracts a specialist delivery vendor against discretionary product opex to ship 2 of the top 3 sales-blocking features within ~5 months — accepting higher unit cost and known integration debt as the price of unblocking pipeline. | reservationPoint | She will not accept any allocation that funds fewer than the equivalent of roughly 2 net-new feature squads dedicated to the sales-blocking gap list for at least 2 consecutive quarters, with protected scope (no in-quarter re-tasking to platform incidents) and a committed GA-readiness path including QA, docs, and enablement — anything thinner is indistinguishable from the status quo that produced the flat ACV trend and is not worth signing. |
| tradeableConcessions | • Accept a hard sunset clause on the acceleration program — funding reverts to the shared pool after 2–3 quarters if pre-agreed leading indicators (qualified pipeline lift, win-rate on competitive deals, design-partner conversion) are not met<br>• Co-fund a defined slice of platform reliability and developer-productivity work out of the acceleration envelope, on the argument that velocity depends on a healthy substrate — giving the platform VP a credible budget line rather than a vague promise<br>• Adopt the new engineering VP's preferred delivery operating model (planning cadence, sizing, dependency management, instrumented DORA-style metrics) for the acceleration squads, so the program becomes the proving ground for her org-wide standards rather than an exception to them<br>• Accept CFO-imposed financial guardrails — a fixed not-to-exceed envelope, monthly burn reporting, and a pre-committed unwind plan if new-ACV does not inflect by a named quarter — in exchange for the program being funded at a level that can actually move the metric<br>• Defer or descope a portion of her own non-acceleration roadmap (lower-ROI feature areas, speculative bets) to free headcount and signal that the ask is incremental investment in the highest-leverage work, not additive on top of an unchanged plan |
| coalitionSignals | • *the-new-vp-of-engineering* — Both need a visible, well-instrumented delivery win early in the new VP's tenure; the acceleration program offers a high-profile vehicle to prove out the new operating model, and the product VP can credibly hand over delivery-discipline authority in exchange for funded scope<br>• *the-cfo* — The CFO's deepest fear is a revenue miss that forces emergency cost actions; a time-boxed, metric-gated acceleration program with a pre-committed unwind clause is a more defensible board narrative than indefinite platform reinvestment with no top-line story, and gives the CFO a structured way to govern spend rather than veto it |

The temptation is to caricature the product VP as a short-termist who wants to mortgage platform health for a feature sugar-rush, ignore tech debt, and pad her own org's headcount — and to dismiss her on the assumption that "velocity won't fix go-to-market problems." That strawman is rejected here. The strongest form of her position starts from a falsifiable business diagnosis (flat new-ACV at enterprise scale is a leading indicator of valuation and headcount risk that dominates every other budget consideration), names a specific causal lever she can be held accountable for (closing a documented sales-blocking feature gap), structures the ask as a time-boxed, metric-gated, unwind-ready program rather than a permanent reallocation, accepts CFO-grade financial guardrails and the new engineering VP's delivery discipline as governance rather than fighting them, and offers concrete concessions on platform co-funding and roadmap descope. Framed this way, her position is not "features over platform" but "a bounded, governed bet on the company's most exposed metric, with explicit off-ramps" — which is harder to refuse on the merits than the strawman version and is the form the CEO is most likely to ratify in a negotiated proposal.

**Steel-man tier:** *strong*

### the-cfo

*Post-IPO finance leader executing a board-mandated margin discipline program that treats infrastructure spend as a strategic lever, not a line-item to defend.*

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| interests | • Restore credibility with public-market investors and the board by demonstrating that operating margin can expand without revenue heroics, since the equity story now depends on durable unit economics rather than growth-at-any-cost<br>• Build a defensible, auditable cost-allocation model that survives external scrutiny (auditors, activist investors, potential M&A diligence) and replaces the opaque "engineering knows best" capital allocation that worked pre-IPO<br>• Protect the company's optionality — preserve enough cash runway and margin headroom that a demand shock, a failed product bet, or a rate-environment change does not force a reactive RIF that destroys far more engineering capacity than a planned 15% efficiency program<br>• Establish a precedent that every function — not just G&A and Sales — participates in margin improvement, because exempting Engineering once means exempting it forever and shifts the entire burden onto headcount actions in other orgs |
| statedPositions | Infrastructure cost must decline 15% YoY enterprise-wide; the engineering budget pool is the primary lever and the mandate is non-negotiable in aggregate, though the path to the number is open for discussion. |
| batna | Escalate to the CEO with a finance-authored allocation that imposes the 15% reduction as a flat top-down cut across all four engineering cost centers, paired with a hiring freeze on backfills and a hard cap on cloud commit renewals. This is materially worse for Engineering than a negotiated plan because it ignores workload heterogeneity, but it is acceptable to the CFO because it still hits the board commitment and shifts the political cost of prioritization onto the engineering VPs. Secondary BATNA: bring in a Big-4 cost-optimization engagement, which will recommend deeper cuts than 15% and damage internal trust further. |
| reservationPoint | Any negotiated outcome must produce a credible, finance-modelable path to at least 12% infrastructure cost reduction in the current fiscal year with a binding commitment to the full 15% by end of next fiscal year, with monthly variance reporting and a clawback mechanism if milestones slip 2 consecutive months. Below 12% in-year, the CFO walks and escalates. |
| tradeableConcessions | • Allow the 15% to be measured on a unit-cost basis (cost per active customer, cost per transaction) rather than absolute dollars, which lets Engineering grow infrastructure in absolute terms if the business grows faster — a meaningful concession because it ties Engineering's autonomy to revenue outcomes finance can model<br>• Fund a one-time capital pool (separate from opex) for FinOps tooling, reserved-instance/commit restructuring, and a dedicated platform-efficiency squad, since these are investments the CFO recognizes as paying back inside 18 months<br>• Extend the timeline from 12 months to 18 months for the full 15%, contingent on a credible quarterly milestone plan with named owners and leading indicators (not lagging cost reports)<br>• Exempt clearly identified strategic-bet workloads (e.g., a named AI/ML initiative tied to the board-approved product roadmap) from the reduction target, capped at a negotiated percentage of total infra spend, in exchange for tighter governance on everything else<br>• Accept that a portion of the savings can be reinvested into engineering productivity tooling rather than flowing entirely to the margin line, provided the net effect on reported opex still meets the board commitment |
| coalitionSignals | • *the-new-vp-of-engineering* — A new VP of Engineering has the strongest incentive of any engineering leader to be seen delivering measurable operational discipline in their first 2 quarters; partnering with Finance on a defensible efficiency plan is a faster credibility win than picking a fight with the CFO, and gives the new VP a mandate to rationalize inherited cost structure that incumbents would defend<br>• *the-product-vp* — Product leadership cares about funding new feature investment and is structurally suspicious of platform/infra spend that does not show up in roadmap velocity; the CFO can credibly argue that efficiency savings free up budget for product bets, making Product a tactical ally against the Platform VP's defense of horizontal infrastructure investment |

The strawman version of the CFO is the green-eyeshade caricature: someone who does not understand engineering, treats infrastructure as a commodity, and is willing to mortgage future capacity for this quarter's margin print. That version is easy to dismiss and produces a bad mediation. The strong form recognizes that a post-IPO CFO with a board-level margin mandate is not improvising — she is executing a strategy that the CEO and board have already endorsed publicly to investors, and her credibility (and the company's equity multiple) depends on hitting it. She is not anti-engineering; she is allocating scarce capital under external constraints the engineering leaders do not personally feel. The strongest form of her position concedes that the path to the number is genuinely open, offers real tradeable concessions (unit-cost framing, timeline flexibility, strategic-bet exemptions, reinvestment of a portion of savings), and uses a BATNA that is unattractive to Engineering specifically because the CFO can credibly execute it without their cooperation. Refusing the strawman means taking seriously that the 15% is a real external commitment, not an opening bid to be split-the-difference negotiated down to 7%.

**Steel-man tier:** *strong*

### the-new-vp-of-engineering

*Newly hired engineering executive treating the current budget dispute as a diagnostic symptom of governance failure and using it as a forcing function to consolidate three siloed platform teams under a unified 2026 platform-of-platforms operating model.*

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| interests | • Establish credibility and durable executive authority in the first 6 months by delivering a visible structural win rather than mediating someone else's turf war<br>• Eliminate the duplicated capacity, overlapping roadmaps, and unclear on-call ownership that are quietly inflating run-rate cost and slowing every product team that depends on platform services<br>• Build a single accountability surface for platform reliability, security posture, and developer-experience metrics so engineering leadership has one throat to choke at the next board review<br>• Prevent the precedent that budget disputes are resolved by horse-trading line items between VPs, which entrenches the silos the consolidation is meant to dissolve<br>• Create the organizational headroom to recruit a strong platform leader under the new model, rather than inheriting three incumbents in a permanent stalemate |
| statedPositions | The 2026 platform-adjacent budgets (infrastructure, developer platform, data platform) should be pooled under a single platform-of-platforms governance model with a unified roadmap, chargeback mechanism, and quarterly portfolio review, rather than allocated line-by-line to the three existing siloed teams. |
| batna | Decline to endorse any line-item allocation, escalate to the CEO with a written memo framing the dispute as evidence the operating model itself is broken, and use the first 2026 quarterly business review to propose consolidation as a standalone reorganization initiative — accepting 1–2 quarters of slower delivery in exchange for not codifying the current silo structure into the FY26 budget baseline. |

| coalitionSignals | • *the-cfo* — Both want to eliminate duplicated spend and gain a defensible unit-economics story for platform investment; a chargeback model and consolidated portfolio review give Finance the cost transparency it has been unable to extract from three independent teams<br>• *the-product-vp* — Product ultimately wants predictable platform delivery and a single intake surface rather than negotiating with three teams whose roadmaps conflict; an SLA-backed consolidated platform is a stronger guarantee than the status quo even if it costs Product some short-term influence over individual platform leads |

The strawman version of this VP is the empire-builder: 60 days in the role and already grabbing budget to inflate her org chart. The steel-man rejects that frame. The strongest reasonable form recognizes that (a) the dispute itself is prima facie evidence the current operating model produces recurring zero-sum conflicts the company keeps paying to mediate; (b) a newly hired VP has both the mandate and the narrow political window to fix structural problems before incumbents recapture the agenda; (c) consolidation is the cheaper long-run answer even if it imposes short-term coordination cost, because duplicated platform capacity at 1,500-person scale silently taxes every product team; and (d) accepting a line-item compromise now locks the silo structure into the FY26 baseline and makes the real fix a year harder. The position is strong precisely because it refuses to treat the budget question as the actual question — the actual question is whether the company keeps paying the governance-gap tax. The concessions list (deferred org moves, protected product commitments, chargeback transparency, internal promotion, SLA-backed accountability) shows this is a negotiator who can give real ground on tactics while holding the structural thesis, which is the opposite of the empire-builder caricature.

**Steel-man tier:** *strong*
