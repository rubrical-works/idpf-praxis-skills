## Educational Scenario

A hypothetical 55-year-old presents with sub-acute (3-day) anterior chest discomfort that worsens with deep inspiration and improves when leaning forward. Mild fever 38.0°C. No prior cardiac history. No identifying details. This is an educational hypothetical for teaching differential reasoning and the discipline of red-flag advocacy.

## Differential Briefs

### Path 1 — Acute pericarditis (mechanistic)

- **Diagnosis:** Acute pericarditis (ICD I30.9)
- **Mechanism:** Inflammation of the pericardial sac produces pain that is sensitive to pericardial position (worse supine, better leaning forward), and often associated with a friction rub on auscultation. Viral etiology is most common in this demographic.
- **Evidence citations:** [systematic-review / cohort / case-series tier examples — illustrative only, not citations to real studies]
- **Test characteristics:** ECG showing diffuse ST elevation with PR depression — sensitive but not specific. Pericardial friction rub — specific (when present) but only 35-85% sensitive across studies. *(LRs vary widely; qualitative ranking is more honest than a single quantitative LR.)*
- **Contraindication reasoning:** Absence of positional component or absence of any inflammatory marker elevation would argue against.

### Path 2 — Community-acquired pneumonia (EBM-tier-walking)

- **Diagnosis:** Community-acquired pneumonia (ICD J18.9)
- **Mechanism:** Alveolar inflammation produces pleuritic pain via parietal pleural inflammation overlying the involved lobe. Fever reflects systemic inflammatory response.
- **Evidence citations:** [evidence-tier-weighted: systematic reviews on CXR sensitivity for CAP; RCT data on empiric antibiotic trials in CAP — illustrative]
- **Test characteristics:** Chest X-ray showing consolidation — published LR+ around 3-5 for "definite CAP" depending on radiologist expertise; LR− around 0.3 in symptomatic patients. CRP — sensitive but non-specific.
- **Contraindication reasoning:** Absence of crackles + normal CXR would argue strongly against.

### Path 3 — Pulmonary embolism (Bayesian-pretest)

- **Diagnosis:** Pulmonary embolism (ICD I26.9)
- **Mechanism:** Thromboembolic obstruction produces V/Q mismatch and pleuritic pain via pulmonary infarct (when peripheral) or right-heart strain.
- **Evidence citations:** [Wells score literature; high-sensitivity D-dimer literature — illustrative tiers]
- **Test characteristics:** High-sensitivity D-dimer — LR− around 0.05 to 0.1 at the standard cutoff in low-Wells-score patients. CT pulmonary angiogram — LR+ around 24, LR− around 0.05 in moderate-Wells-score patients.
- **Contraindication reasoning:** Positional component favoring pericarditis and clear infectious markers favor CAP — both lower the PE pre-test probability.

---

## Red-Flag Advocate

**Must-not-miss diagnosis:** Pulmonary embolism

**Sharing-features rationale:** Pleuritic chest pain and dyspnea overlap with both pericarditis and CAP. PE can present without classic risk factors (the "Wells score 0-1" subgroup is non-negligible). Tachycardia — if it were present in the scenario — would shift the prior upward.

**Ruling-out test:** High-sensitivity D-dimer (qualitative impact: strong-rule-out in low-Wells-score patients). When the test is unavailable or pre-test probability is high, CT pulmonary angiogram remains the reference standard.

**Consequence of missing severity:** `catastrophic` — untreated PE can produce sudden cardiovascular collapse and death; the "miss" outcome is a known cause of preventable mortality.

The red-flag advocate is mandatory regardless of how compelling pericarditis appears as the leading diagnosis. Even if the leading diagnosis is correct, *always* asking "what's the must-not-miss?" is the cognitive discipline being taught.

## Bayesian Pre/Post-Test Synthesis

### Pre-test probabilities

| Differential | Pre-test probability | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Acute pericarditis | ~0.40 | Assumption-based — published prevalence in this presentation varies; mid-range estimate given the positional component. |
| Community-acquired pneumonia | ~0.25 | Assumption-based — moderate prior in this age group with subacute fever. |
| Pulmonary embolism | ~0.10 | Literature-cited — Wells-score 0-1 subgroup with pleuritic pain has reported prevalence ~5-15% across emergency-department cohorts. |

### Highest-impact test

**Test:** High-sensitivity D-dimer.
**Target differentials:** Pulmonary embolism (rule out); pericarditis and CAP unaffected.
**Likelihood ratios:** LR− approximately 0.05 to 0.1 in low-Wells-score patients (literature-cited).
**Why this test:** A negative result at low pre-test probability reduces PE post-test probability to a clinically negligible range, allowing the workup to focus on pericarditis vs CAP. A positive result raises PE probability enough to justify CT pulmonary angiogram, the more expensive next step.

### Post-test probability landscape

| Differential | Positive D-dimer post-test | Negative D-dimer post-test |
|---|---|---|
| Acute pericarditis | ~0.40 (unchanged) | ~0.40 (unchanged) |
| Community-acquired pneumonia | ~0.25 (unchanged) | ~0.25 (unchanged) |
| Pulmonary embolism | rises (further workup needed) | ~0.005 (effectively ruled out) |

### Educational Learning Objective

A negative high-sensitivity D-dimer in a low-pre-test-probability patient is a strong rule-out (LR− ≈ 0.05). The remaining workup is between pericarditis and CAP — both of which have similar pleuritic-pain presentations and need different first-line tests (ECG/echocardiogram for pericarditis; CXR for CAP). The Bayesian intuition being taught: *high-sensitivity tests are for ruling out, not ruling in.* A negative D-dimer does not tell us pericarditis is the answer — it tells us PE is not.

## Educational Learning Summary

The red-flag-advocate discipline catches PE — a low-pre-test-probability diagnosis that nevertheless carries catastrophic-if-missed consequences. The Bayesian synthesis names the right *next* test (D-dimer) rather than the *most informative-in-isolation* test (CT pulmonary angiogram). The case teaches the difference between "the diagnosis with highest pre-test probability" and "the diagnosis you must rule out first," and demonstrates that the cheapest sensitive test at low pre-test probability is the rational next step.
